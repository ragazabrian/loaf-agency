Purpose, Bias + Strategic Intent

Roots of behaviour

Head of strategy – Sarah Joynt-Bowe · 10 July 2020

Purpose is the answer. Purpose is dead. Who cares about purpose? All we care about is purpose. When Jim Stengel laid out the idea of ‘Brand Ideal’ in his book Grow, the advertising industry bought-in for a pound. Surely this was the silver bullet. The best way to build emotional resonance and get customers to associate the brand with all that glitters and the gold. 

Unfortunately, most of the people who bought-in saw it as a business tool, rather than a passion-filled, action-fueling life force. On Stengal’s website, he proudly declares: “The surest path to growth – pursue an aim greater than profit.” This in itself is absurd—the surest path to growth—measured by profit—is to pursue an aim greater than profit. So, if we want to transform stagnant brands, all we need an altruistic band-aid. 

Behavioural science specialist Richard Shotton categorised this bias as ‘wishful seeing’ in his book, The Choice Factory.  “Agencies have been guilty of promoting theories of advertising that they want to be true,” wrote Shotton, “rather than ones that actually are true.” And the beauty of brand purpose is that it is actually both. It can be a vehicle for wishful seeing, but it can also be an incredibly powerful motivator for consumers. 

The trick is not aligning your purpose with your values, it's aligning your purpose with your product. Having a broader goal is only effective when it matches with the goals of your end customer. By self-identifying as a member of the target audience, rather than an external force, every action taken to boost the confidence of the audience, boosts the confidence in the brand. Sociologist William Sumner noted that “each group nourishes its own pride and vanity, boasts itself superior, exists in its own divinities, and looks with contempt on outsiders,” and while this notion is historically at the root of most political conflicts, we, as wishful seeing advertisers, can see the value in grounding strategies in this theory. 

Behaviour is at the root of any great communications strategy. Our messaging goals are always rooted in prompting, halting, or shifting a behaviour. Where we, as advertisers, need to do better, is in understanding the product, independent of its grand ambitions or sordid past, to understand why it was created. If we dig and dig and dig— and at the bottom of the pile—still find pure profit and practicality, perhaps it’s time to shelf brand purpose for that case.
