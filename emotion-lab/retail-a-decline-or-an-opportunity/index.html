A decline or an opportunity?

Retail destinations...

MD/Co-founder – Alan Houghton · 10 October 2020

The decline of the highstreet has been well reported. The stats are clear but what isn’t is the cause. I’ve recently spent some time thinking about placemaking and the future of retail, and there are some fundamental insights and solutions out there. 

What can we do to keep bricks-and-mortar shopping desirable and who is to blame for this decline in the high st? There is no doubt that it’s heavily impacted by the surge of online, but it’s the place that holds the power. Retailers want to be in desirable places that offer a sound business case. Town centres across the country need to develop a place that people actually want to visit. I recently heard how Barnsley town centre is working with architects who have a clear vision for their future. They’re working to develop the downtrodden and unattractive town centre and create a place where it is an enjoyable experience to visit. 

With a strong high street, once you add in a leisure centre and a library, a sense of community is borne. There’s a meeting place for people to love, a place for people to belong, and a place that’s unique to the people of the area. More needs to be done by the councils to show a greater vision. They need to be responsible for placemaking. And it is the retailers task of making their offering something people want to come and experience, across all consumer touchpoints. From the moment they walk in, they should be immersed in the brand, not just the products they sell. Customer service and interactions are key to creating memorability and recall and these are things that some of the more well known high st brands have failed to embrace and they have since suffered the consequences. 

There are leaders in the pack that retailers can look to for inspiration. Adidas has a shop/workspace in London diversifying their offer and creating a meeting place and reason to visit the shop that’s not just about buying their products. Rapha has introduced a clubhouse space to hang out in their London store. Both of these activities speak directly to the values and community-building goals of the brands. It’s about experiencing the brand, and its values, across every touchpoint. There have also been moves over the last few years by companies such as Mercedes - building experiential stores in retail spaces not typically associated with the automotive industry, that allow potential customers that would never walk into a showroom, to experience Mercedes products and services with no extra effort. It is a real glimpse into how these spaces could develop in the future.

Also, we can’t forget about the independents. The NQ in Manchester is the 2nd most visited place in the city centre with its vast choice of shops, restaurants, and bars all offering a nicer experience to the chains that you may get elsewhere in the city. Independents excel at defining their brand purpose and creating desirable spaces for their target consumers because they have to. It’s the key to distinctiveness. It is great to see so many of these independents now making a mark on the retail landscape. 

So, is it as bleak as it seems? The numbers aren’t proving as sensational as the headlines promise but this trend should be seen as an opportunity. It's about diversification. It’s about using the physical space to its full potential. It’s about innovating and looking to the future. What you cannot do online is interact, spend time, shop and eat with friends and family, but most importantly touch, feel, taste and try for yourself. It's about the right mix of activity. Every brand should embrace the physical and digital activations of their brand, to ensure that each and every time a consumer comes into contact you’re adding to their overall experience. It's about knowing who you are, what your brand stands for and why consumers should care - then offering an experience that matches that. It’s down to the retailers, and the places themselves, to innovate and evolve to match consumer demand, whilst offering difference, and new reasons to visit - some of which the consumer won’t even know they want yet.

As the saying goes: what got you here, won’t get you there.
